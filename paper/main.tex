\begin{abstract}
Passive acoustic monitoring (PAM) produces far more audio than experts can
annotate by hand, which motivates automatic species recognition from field
recordings. We present a multi-label bioacoustic classifier for the BirdCLEF
dataset, which covers birds, amphibians, insects, mammals and reptiles of the
Brazilian Pantanal. Recordings are split into 5-second clips and turned into
3-channel log-mel/delta/delta-delta spectrograms. Each clip is encoded by a
compact ResNet-style CNN, and a \emph{class-wise attention pooling} layer
combines the clips into one recording-level prediction. Training follows a
three-phase curriculum that moves from a family-balanced, clean-first subset to
the full dataset including soundscapes. The data is heavily imbalanced: 169
labelled classes, a median of 11 examples per class, a maximum of 666, and
67.7\% of records are birds. On the internal validation split the model reaches
a macro ROC-AUC of 0.5982, a macro PR-AUC of 0.0388, a rare-class ROC-AUC of
0.6236 and a family-level ROC-AUC of 0.6987. On the soundscape-style holdout
test set these become 0.4770, 0.2544, 0.4661 and 0.4248. Below-chance holdout
scores for head classes (0.3671), Amphibia (0.3333) and Mammalia (0.0243) point
to a systematic domain shift between curated, bird-dominated focal recordings
and amphibian-dominated soundscapes, not just weak learning. We report every
result produced by the final run, analyse the model's attention behaviour, list
the inconsistencies we found in the experimental record, and interpret what the
results do and do not show.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Ecosystems worldwide face unprecedented pressure, and effective conservation
increasingly depends on monitoring biodiversity at scale. In remote,
ecologically rich regions, direct observation is costly and sparse. Passive
acoustic monitoring offers an alternative: autonomous recorders listen to an
ecosystem continuously and capture the vocal activity of many taxa over long
periods. The bottleneck then moves from collecting data to \emph{analysing}
it. Thousands of hours of audio cannot be labelled manually, so automatic,
scalable recognition methods are needed.

This study focuses on the Pantanal wetlands of Brazil, one of the largest and
most biodiverse wetland systems on Earth. It covers more than
150{,}000~km$^2$ and is home to over 650 bird species and many other vocalising
taxa. Much of the Pantanal remains under-monitored because of its scale,
seasonal flooding, and growing threats from wildfire, agricultural expansion
and climate change.

We study deep transfer-style learning for this setting using the BirdCLEF
dataset, which combines focal recordings from citizen-science platforms
(iNaturalist, Xeno-canto) with continuous soundscape recordings. These data are
\emph{weakly labelled}: a label applies to a whole recording, not to the moment
a species calls, and several species may be present at once. The class
distribution is also strongly long-tailed and taxonomically skewed towards
birds.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A complete preprocessing and modelling pipeline that combines 3-channel
        spectral--temporal inputs, a lightweight ResNet-style clip encoder,
        and class-wise attention pooling for multiple-instance aggregation
        over clips (Section~\ref{sec:methods}).
  \item A three-phase curriculum (family-balanced $\rightarrow$
        class-aware $\rightarrow$ natural with class-weighted loss) designed
        to counter class and taxonomic imbalance
        (Section~\ref{sec:training}).
  \item A full report of all dataset statistics, overall, subgroup,
        family-level, training-dynamics and attention results
        (Sections~\ref{sec:data}--\ref{sec:results}).
  \item An interpretation of the large validation-to-holdout gap, including
        below-chance AUCs, metric-prevalence effects and data-composition
        shift, plus an audit of inconsistencies in the experimental record
        (Sections~\ref{sec:interp}--\ref{sec:audit}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Problem Statement}

Let a recording $r$ be split into $T$ clips $X_r=\{x_1,\dots,x_T\}$, and let
$\mathbf{y}_r\in\{0,1\}^C$ be its multi-hot label vector over $C$ classes. We
learn $f:X_r\mapsto\mathbf{p}_r\in[0,1]^C$, where $p_{r,c}$ estimates the
probability that class $c$ is present \emph{somewhere} in the recording. This
is a multiple-instance learning (MIL) problem: the label is attached to the
bag (recording), not the instances (clips).

Three properties make the problem hard:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Long-tailed classes.} Few classes have many examples; most have
        very few (Figure~\ref{fig:classdist}).
  \item \textbf{Taxonomic skew and domain shift.} Focal recordings are mostly
        birds, while soundscapes are mostly amphibians and insects
        (Section~\ref{sec:data}).
  \item \textbf{Weak and noisy labels.} Recording-level labels, optional and
        incomplete secondary labels, and quality ratings that vary widely.
\end{itemize}
Left unaddressed, imbalance makes a model learn the dominant bird classes well
and the rare taxa poorly, which is the opposite of what biodiversity
monitoring needs.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Sources}
We use the BirdCLEF data release, which has three parts: (i)
\texttt{train.csv}, metadata for focal recordings in \texttt{train\_audio/};
(ii) \texttt{train\_soundscapes\_labels.csv}, with semicolon-separated
multi-species labels for continuous soundscapes; and (iii)
\texttt{taxonomy.csv}, which maps each label to scientific name, common name
and taxonomic class. \texttt{train.csv} has \textbf{35{,}549 rows and 15
columns}. Non-informative columns (\texttt{collection}, \texttt{author},
\texttt{license}, \texttt{url}, \texttt{inat\_taxon\_id},
\texttt{common\_name}, \texttt{type}) were dropped. The remaining columns are
primary label, secondary labels, latitude, longitude, scientific name, class
name, rating, filename and a derived full path (35{,}549 $\times$ 9; 35{,}549
$\times$ 11 after adding the parsed label list and encoded target). Secondary
labels were parsed from their string form with \texttt{ast.literal\_eval}. The
full label set of a record is its primary label followed by its secondary
labels, and the target is a multi-hot vector over the union of species in
\texttt{train.csv} and the soundscape labels.

Each record is assigned to a \emph{phase group} by its user rating:
\textbf{clean} (rating $=5$), \textbf{semi} ($2.5\le$ rating $<5$),
\textbf{noisy} (rating $<2.5$), and \textbf{soundscape} (continuous field
recordings). These groups drive the curriculum (Section~\ref{sec:training}).

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Audio preprocessing}
All recordings are converted to mono by averaging channels and resampled to
32{,}000~Hz. Each recording is split into non-overlapping 5-second clips,
\begin{equation}
  X=\{x_1,x_2,\dots,x_T\},\qquad x_t\in\mathbb{R}^{160{,}000},
\end{equation}
with zero-padding on the final clip when needed. At most 8 clips per recording
are used ($T\le 8$).

\subsection{Spectral representation}
Each clip becomes a 3-channel stack of log-mel, delta and delta-delta
spectrograms. The STFT is computed as
\begin{equation}
  X[k,\tau]=\sum_n x[n]\,w[n-\tau H]\,e^{-j2\pi kn/N},\qquad P[k,\tau]=|X[k,\tau]|^2,
\end{equation}
with $N=n_{\text{fft}}=2048$ and hop $H=512$. It is projected onto 128 mel
bands between $f_{\min}=300$~Hz and $f_{\max}=16{,}000$~Hz,
\begin{equation}
  M[m,\tau]=\sum_k B[m,k]\,P[k,\tau],\qquad
  S[m,\tau]=10\log_{10}\!\big(\max(M[m,\tau],\epsilon)\big),
\end{equation}
and resized to a fixed $128\times384$ resolution. The delta channels are
first- and second-order temporal derivatives of $S$, computed with the usual
regression formula
\begin{equation}
  \Delta S[m,\tau]=\frac{\sum_{n=1}^{K} n\,(S[m,\tau+n]-S[m,\tau-n])}{2\sum_{n=1}^K n^2},
  \qquad \Delta^2 S=\Delta(\Delta S).
\end{equation}
Figure~\ref{fig:input} shows an example. The log-mel channel shows harmonic
banding (energy concentrated between bins $\sim$15--50 and a stationary band
near bin 90--100). The delta channels emphasise onsets and transients as
vertical striations, which makes temporal structure explicit to the CNN.

log-mel spectrogram (left), first-order delta (centre) and second-order delta

\paragraph{Normalisation.} Each channel is standardised independently per
frequency bin across time:
\begin{equation}
  \mu_f=\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T}S_{f,t},\quad
  \sigma_f=\sqrt{\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T}(S_{f,t}-\mu_f)^2+\epsilon},\quad
  \tilde S_{f,t}=\frac{S_{f,t}-\mu_f}{\sigma_f}.
\end{equation}
Per-bin normalisation removes stationary spectral colouring (microphone
response, constant background hum), much like cepstral mean--variance
normalisation in speech.

\subsection{Clip encoder}
Each clip $x_t\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times128\times384}$ is encoded by a
ResNet-style CNN $f_\theta$ into an embedding $h_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ with
$d=96$:
\begin{equation}
  h_t=\mathrm{Dropout}_{0.2}\big(\mathrm{GAP}(F_\theta(x_t))\big).
\end{equation}
The stem is a $7\times7$ stride-2 convolution, then batch normalisation, ReLU
and $3\times3$ max pooling. Residual stages follow, each built from blocks of
two $3\times3$ convolution--BN--ReLU layers with identity skip connections.
Each stage starts with a strided convolution, so spatial resolution halves
while channel width grows in the standard ResNet progression from a base width
of 24. Global average pooling (GAP) over frequency and time produces the clip
embedding. The 3-channel input lines up with RGB-shaped CNN stems and lets the
first layer learn joint spectro-temporal filters.

\subsection{Class-wise attention pooling}
To turn clip embeddings into a recording-level prediction without timestamps,
we use class-wise attention MIL pooling. For each class $c$, a small attention
network $g_c$ scores each clip:
\begin{equation}
  e_{t,c}=g_c(h_t),\qquad
  \alpha_{t,c}=\frac{\exp(e_{t,c})}{\sum_{t'=1}^{T}\exp(e_{t',c})},\qquad
  \sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{t,c}=1.
\end{equation}
Padded clips are excluded through a clip mask. Each class gets its own pooled
representation and logit:
\begin{equation}
  z_c=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{t,c}\,h_t,\qquad
  \hat y_c=\mathbf{w}_c^{\top}z_c+b_c,\qquad
  p_c=\sigma(\hat y_c)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\hat y_c}}.
\end{equation}
Unlike mean pooling, which weights every clip equally, or a single shared
attention vector, the class-wise formulation lets different species depend on
different parts of the same recording. In effect it acts as a weak event
detector. The model also returns per-clip (instance) logits and probabilities,
bag-level probabilities, clip and bag embeddings, and the attention matrix
$\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times C}$, which we use for interpretation
(Section~\ref{sec:attention}).

\subsection{Training procedure: three-phase curriculum}
\label{sec:training}
Training follows a curriculum that moves from clean, balanced data to the full
natural distribution.

\begin{description}
  \item[Phase 1 (family-balanced).] Target of 20 examples per family
        (five families, about 100 recordings). Examples are drawn
        clean-first: clean $\rightarrow$ semi $\rightarrow$ noisy
        $\rightarrow$ soundscape, the next group being used only when the
        previous one cannot meet the quota. Loss: unweighted BCE. Learning
        rate $10^{-3}$.
  \item[Phase 2 (class-aware).] Clean + semi + noisy data, sampled with class
        weights
        \begin{equation}
          w_c\propto\frac{1}{\sqrt{\text{class\_count}_c}},
        \end{equation}
        a square-root inverse-frequency compromise between natural and fully
        balanced sampling. Loss: unweighted BCE. Learning rate
        $5\times10^{-4}$.
  \item[Phase 3 (natural + weighted loss).] Full dataset including
        soundscapes, shuffled naturally. Loss: class-weighted BCE,
        \begin{equation}
          \mathcal L=\frac1C\sum_{c=1}^{C}\Big[-\beta_c\,y_c\log p_c-(1-y_c)\log(1-p_c)\Big],
        \end{equation}
        where $\beta_c$ up-weights positives of infrequent classes.
        Learning rate $10^{-4}$.
\end{description}

Optimisation uses AdamW (weight decay $10^{-4}$) with batch size 1 (one
recording, i.e.\ one bag of up to 8 clips, per step). No data augmentation is
used in the final run, so the effect of the curriculum and architecture can be
seen in isolation.

\subsection{Validation, stopping and model selection}
A 10\% internal validation split, stratified by soundscape presence where
possible, is used for model selection and plateau detection. Each phase runs
until validation macro ROC-AUC fails to improve by at least $0.001$ for a
fixed number of consecutive epochs (the experimental record states both 6 and
4; see Section~\ref{sec:audit}). The checkpoint with the best validation macro
ROC-AUC across all phases is kept. A separate holdout test set, closer to
continuous soundscape recordings, is used only for final evaluation. All
results come from a \emph{single} final training run, not cross-validation.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Macro ROC-AUC}: the mean over classes of the one-vs-rest ROC
        AUC, computed over classes with at least one positive example. Chance
        level is 0.5.
  \item \textbf{Macro PR-AUC}: the mean over classes of average precision. The
        chance level for class $c$ equals its prevalence $\pi_c$, so the
        macro chance level depends on the evaluation set.
  \item \textbf{Rare-class ROC-AUC}: macro ROC-AUC over low-frequency classes.
  \item \textbf{Family-level ROC-AUC}: species probabilities are max-pooled
        within each family, $p_{\text{fam}}=\max_{c\in\text{fam}}p_c$, and
        ROC-AUC is computed per family.
  \item \textbf{Subgroups}: \emph{head / medium / tail} (tertiles of
        training frequency), \emph{rare}, \emph{missing-from-clean} (absent
        from the clean subset), and \emph{soundscape-heavy} ($\ge50\%$ of
        samples from soundscapes).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Earlier pipeline iteration (repository code)}
The repository also contains an earlier pipeline iteration, which is not the
model behind the results reported here. That pipeline uses an
ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18 with a 1-channel stem and a BN--Dropout--Linear
head (\texttt{BirdResNet}). Its log-mel settings are $n_{\text{fft}}=1024$,
hop 320, 128 mels over 20--14{,}000~Hz. It supports signal-centred, sequential,
random, ``WHEN''-detector and hybrid chunking, and it trains with BCE whose
positive weights are $\text{neg}_c/\text{pos}_c$ clipped to $[1,20]$. Its
optional ``WHEN'' detector is a small CRNN (three conv--BN--ReLU blocks with
32/64/128 channels, frequency max-pooling, and a bidirectional GRU with hidden
size 128) that predicts frame-level event activity. No evaluation results for
this iteration are available, so it is described here only for completeness.

The final run used AdamW, batch size 1, encoder base width 24, embedding
dimension 96, dropout 0.2, at most 8 clips per sequence, the three-phase
curriculum, and no augmentation. The best checkpoint by validation macro
ROC-AUC was kept.

\section{Interpretation}
\label{sec:interp}

\subsection{The model learns something, but much of it does not transfer}
On validation, all ROC metrics sit clearly above 0.5, from 0.5982 macro to
0.6987 family-level, so the network extracts real discriminative information.
On holdout data, every ROC metric falls below 0.5 (0.4770 macro, 0.4661 rare,
0.4248 family). A truly uninformative model would score about 0.5. Scores
consistently \emph{below} 0.5 mean the model ranks negatives above positives
more often than chance would, so the cues it learned are \emph{anti-correlated}
with the labels on the holdout distribution. This is the signature of
\textbf{shortcut learning under domain shift}, not of simple underfitting.

\subsection{Taxonomy is confounded with recording condition}
Table~\ref{tab:phasefamily} suggests why. Birds are almost entirely focal
recordings (clean, semi, noisy). Amphibians, insects and reptiles are almost
entirely soundscapes. A model can reduce its training loss by learning ``what
a soundscape sounds like'' (background noise floor, broadband stationary
energy, recorder characteristics) as a proxy for ``non-bird taxon'', and
``what a focal recording sounds like'' (high-SNR foreground calls) as a proxy
for ``bird''. Several results fit this reading:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Strong validation family scores for non-birds} (Reptilia
        0.9494, Insecta 0.8725, Amphibia 0.8054) and for the soundscape-heavy
        group (0.7333). In the internal split, recognising the recording
        \emph{condition} is enough to separate these families.
  \item \textbf{Weak validation score for Aves (0.3476).} When almost every
        non-soundscape recording is a bird, ``is there a bird?'' carries
        little information at the family level once probabilities are
        max-pooled over $\sim$hundreds of bird species. Max-pooling also
        inflates the family score of every recording, which hurts ranking.
  \item \textbf{On the soundscape-style holdout, the shortcut breaks.} When
        every holdout recording is a soundscape, the condition cue no longer
        separates families. Amphibia drops by 0.4721 and Insecta by 0.3644.
        Aves is the only family that \emph{improves} (+0.1466 to 0.4942),
        which fits a model that had been actively penalising bird
        probabilities in soundscape-like audio. Removing that confound moves
        Aves back towards chance.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Below-chance extremes and small-sample variance}
The holdout family values 0.3333 (Amphibia) and 0.0243 (Mammalia) deserve
particular care. An ROC-AUC of exactly $1/3$ is what one gets with very small
positive/negative counts (for example one positive ranked against three
negatives), and family-level AUC on a holdout of soundscape files is computed
over relatively few recordings. Mammalia has only 98 training records in total
and 50 in soundscapes. An AUC of 0.0243 means the ranking is almost perfectly
\emph{inverted}: flipping the scores would give 0.9757. The model does
separate mammal-positive from mammal-negative holdout recordings, but in the
wrong direction, which again points to a learned cue whose sign flips between
domains. With a single run and no confidence intervals, these extremes should
be read as strong qualitative evidence of shift, not as precise estimates.

\subsection{Why PR-AUC goes \emph{up} while ROC-AUC goes down}
Macro PR-AUC rises from 0.0388 to 0.2544 even though ROC-AUC falls. This is
not a contradiction. A random classifier's PR-AUC equals the positive
prevalence. The validation split covers up to 169 classes with typically one
label per recording (prevalence on the order of $1/169\approx0.006$ per class),
so 0.0388 is several times chance. Soundscape holdout files are multi-label,
and macro averaging covers only the subset of classes present, so prevalence
per evaluated class is much higher and the chance level of PR-AUC rises with
it. \textbf{PR-AUC values from the two sets therefore cannot be compared
directly.} The holdout 0.2544 should be read against the holdout prevalence,
and with ROC-AUC below 0.5 it is likely \emph{at or below} the holdout chance
level.

\subsection{Imbalance handling: partial success on validation}
On validation, the less-represented groups score better than head classes:
rare 0.6236 and tail 0.6091 versus head 0.5850, and the rare-class score
exceeds the overall macro score of 0.5982. This is the direction the
curriculum was built to produce. Family-balanced Phase 1, square-root
class-aware sampling in Phase 2 and positive-weighted BCE in Phase 3 all
shift gradient mass towards tail classes. Within the training distribution,
the imbalance countermeasures appear to work.

On the holdout set, however, tail (0.4661), rare (0.4661) and
missing-from-clean (0.4678) classes all fall to just below chance, so the
benefit does not survive the domain shift.

\subsection{Why medium classes win and head classes lose on holdout}
Medium-frequency classes are the only group to improve (0.6174
$\rightarrow$ 0.6935), while head classes collapse (0.5850 $\rightarrow$
0.3671). The most frequent classes are mostly birds with large clean/semi
focal collections (Table~\ref{tab:clean}). These are the classes most exposed
to the focal-recording shortcut, so their representations are tied to
foreground, high-SNR conditions that do not occur in soundscapes. The
positive weighting and class-aware sampling also push head-class logits
\emph{down} relative to the rest, which is useful under imbalance but harmful
when those classes appear in a new domain. Medium-frequency classes have
enough data to learn real acoustic patterns, yet are less dominated by any
single recording condition, which gives them the best mix of signal and
robustness. Tail classes simply have too few examples (median class size 11)
to learn condition-invariant features.

\subsection{What the training dynamics show}
In the two recorded Phase-1 epochs, training loss falls sharply (0.205
$\rightarrow$ 0.063) and lands on the validation loss (0.061), while
\emph{train} ROC-AUC stays flat at about 0.489. A falling BCE with flat,
sub-chance ranking is typical of the early ``predict-all-negative'' phase of
multi-label training: with roughly 169 mostly-zero targets per example, the
model first learns to push every output towards the base rate, which
minimises BCE without discriminating between classes. Validation ROC-AUC
falling from 0.598 to 0.484 over the same step shows the epoch-1 value is not
a stable estimate. Its size is plausible for an almost-untrained network
evaluated on a small split, and it should be treated with caution. The
reported Phase-3 peak of 0.6783 after 24 epochs shows that real
discrimination emerges later, once the full dataset and weighted loss are
used.

\subsection{What the attention map shows}
The attention example illustrates both the promise and the current weakness
of class-wise pooling. The mechanism does what it is meant to do: different
classes prefer different clips (four each way). Three warning signs appear:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Low selectivity.} Weights range from 0.37 to 0.63, close to
        uniform (0.5). The model is not yet acting as a sharp event detector;
        in practice it is close to mean pooling.
  \item \textbf{Attention to a mostly padded clip.} clip\_1 is about 72\%
        zero padding, yet it gets the \emph{highest} weight in the map (0.63
        for 24279) and is preferred by half the classes. A spectrogram region
        of padding, which after per-bin normalisation produces strongly
        atypical values, can act as a spurious feature. The final clip of
        nearly every recording is partly padded, so this is a likely
        systematic artefact.
  \item \textbf{Wrong predictions.} The true species (\texttt{rumfly1}, a
        bird with 77 clean examples, the second-largest clean class) is not in
        the top 8. The top predictions are dominated by soundscape-heavy
        non-bird taxa. This matches the confounding hypothesis: the
        noisy-looking spectrogram of clip\_0 (broadband vertical transients,
        diffuse background) resembles soundscape audio more than a clean
        focal recording.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Overall assessment}
The final system is a well-motivated architecture whose training-distribution
behaviour (above-chance ROC everywhere, strong family-level separation, tail
classes outperforming head classes) shows the design choices have some
effect. Its holdout behaviour shows that, as trained, it relies heavily on
recording-condition cues that are confounded with taxonomy, and those cues
reverse sign in deployment-like soundscapes. The main obstacle is not model
capacity but \textbf{the confound between domain and label in the training
data}, made worse by weak labels, no augmentation, and a single run with no
uncertainty estimates.

\section{Consistency Audit of the Experimental Record}
\label{sec:audit}
To report results faithfully, we list the discrepancies found when comparing
the run summary, the exported training history and the repository code.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Headline metric versus best checkpoint.} The reported
        validation macro ROC-AUC (0.5982) and PR-AUC (0.0388) match the
        \emph{epoch-1, Phase-1} values in \texttt{epoch\_history.jsonl}
        (0.598242 and 0.038834) to the reported precision. The run summary
        also states that the best checkpoint came from Phase 3 at 0.6783. If
        selection maximised validation ROC-AUC across phases, the headline
        validation figure should be at least 0.6783. Either the headline
        validation metrics were taken from the wrong row, or the evaluated
        checkpoint is the epoch-1 model. In the second case the holdout
        numbers describe an almost-untrained network. Which is true cannot be
        settled from the available artefacts, and it materially changes how
        the holdout numbers should be read.
  \item \textbf{Incomplete history.} The exported history holds only two
        epochs, although Phase 3 alone is reported to have run for 24 epochs.
  \item \textbf{Patience.} The methods description specifies a plateau
        patience of 6 epochs; the training-setup description specifies 4.
  \item \textbf{Spectrogram settings.} The final run uses
        $n_{\text{fft}}=2048$, hop 512, 300--16{,}000~Hz. The repository's
        \texttt{config.py} specifies $n_{\text{fft}}=1024$, hop 320,
        20--14{,}000~Hz, and the inference helper hard-codes hop 512 and
        $f_{\max}=8000$~Hz. The class-wise attention model and its sequence
        dataset (\texttt{BirdCLEFSpectrogramModel},
        \texttt{sequence\_dataset}) are not in the repository, which
        contains only the earlier ResNet-18 pipeline.
  \item \textbf{Class histogram range.} The summary statistics of per-class
        counts report a maximum of 666, whereas the class-distribution
        histogram (Figure~\ref{fig:classdist}) has non-empty bins near
        1{,}100. The plotted call passes the whole two-column count table to
        the histogram, so the figure may not exactly match the statistics.
  \item \textbf{Rare versus tail.} Holdout rare-class and tail ROC-AUC are
        identical (0.4661) while their validation values differ (0.6236 vs.\
        0.6091). This suggests the two groups coincide on the holdout set,
        or that one value was copied.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations and Future Work}

\paragraph{Limitations.} (i) A single training run with no cross-validation
or confidence intervals. (ii) No data augmentation. (iii) Weak
recording-level labels with incomplete secondary labels. (iv) Severe
confounding between taxonomy and recording condition. (v) A fixed 5-second
clip representation with at most 8 clips, so long-range temporal context is
lost and the last clip is often mostly padding. (vi) The inconsistencies
listed in Section~\ref{sec:audit}.

\paragraph{Future work.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Fix and re-evaluate.} Re-export the full per-epoch history,
        confirm which checkpoint was evaluated, re-run holdout evaluation
        with the Phase-3 best checkpoint, and report bootstrap confidence
        intervals and per-class positive counts.
  \item \textbf{Break the confound.} Mix soundscape background into focal
        bird recordings and focal non-bird calls into clean backgrounds, add
        background-noise injection, SpecAugment-style time/frequency masking
        and mixup, so that recording condition stops predicting taxonomy.
  \item \textbf{Mask padding.} Exclude padded frames from normalisation and
        attention, or replace zero padding with loop or reflection padding.
  \item \textbf{Sharper attention.} Use attention temperature or entropy
        regularisation, or auxiliary instance-level losses, to encourage
        selective event localisation.
  \item \textbf{Domain adaptation and pseudo-labelling.} Use unlabelled
        soundscapes through self-training or adversarial domain alignment;
        evaluate with prevalence-aware metrics such as padded cmAP.
  \item \textbf{Stronger encoders.} Use pretrained audio backbones and
        sequence models over clip embeddings to capture longer context.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
We built a CNN-based bioacoustic classifier with 3-channel spectral--temporal
inputs, class-wise attention pooling and a three-phase imbalance-aware
curriculum for the long-tailed, multi-taxon BirdCLEF Pantanal dataset. On
internal validation it reaches a macro ROC-AUC of 0.5982 (0.6783 Phase-3
peak), a rare-class ROC-AUC of 0.6236, a family-level ROC-AUC of 0.6987, and
per-family scores up to 0.9494 (Reptilia), with tail classes outperforming
head classes. On the soundscape-style holdout set, performance falls to a
macro ROC-AUC of 0.4770, rare-class 0.4661 and family-level 0.4248. Only
medium-frequency classes (0.6935) and Aves (+0.1466) improve. The systematic
below-chance scores, together with the near-total confounding of taxonomy and
recording condition in the training data, point to domain shift and shortcut
learning as the main failure mode. Closing the gap between curated recordings
and real soundscapes, through condition-breaking augmentation, padding-aware
attention, domain adaptation and a careful re-evaluation of the final
checkpoint, is the clearest path to a deployable monitoring system.
